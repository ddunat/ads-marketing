## 1. The engine: the Ad Kaleidoscope (how we spend effort)
- **Volume beats luck.** Make many ads, find the few that win, then put **80% of effort into variations of winners** and 20% into hunting the next winner.
- **Retire an ad when the numbers say it's tired, not when we are.** We tire of an ad long before the market has seen it. "New" means new to the viewer, not new from scratch.
- **Remix** (edit only, fast, wears out faster). Easiest first:
  1. speed 1.1x
  2. filter
  3. background or border
  4. fonts and captions
  5. headline overlay (top third)
  6. new medium (video hook → static image)
  7. new format (9:16 → 1:1 / 4:5 per placement)
  8. same hook with a different middle section
  9. effects early in the hook
- **Remake** (re-record the same winning script; lasts months). Easiest first:
  1. clone it (re-shoot as is)
  2. new props
  3. new examples (swap the suburb or the defect)
  4. new setting (a different site)
  5. new talent who looks like the buyer
  6. combinations of these
- **For DC:**
  - Every Ad Desk winner gets a kaleidoscope batch. HeyGen makes remakes cheap: same script with a new look (David's navy, white or blue shirt), a new site background, a new example suburb.
  - Every winning video hook also becomes 2–4 static images (the book's statics from winning hooks won too).

## 2. The Best Ad checklist: every DC ad must pass it before Jeremy sees it
**What they see (visual)**
- Must have:
  - a visual hook paired with the spoken hook
  - movement: walking on site, a prop, a fast cut, motion in the first second
  - a presenter who looks like the buyer
  - **show what you tell** (if we say "crack", we show the crack)
  - burned-in captions
  - platform-native formats (9:16 for reels and stories, 4:5 for feed)
- Nice to have:
  - a trending format (podcast-style clip, selfie walk-and-talk)
  - real demonstrations: moisture meter, thermal camera, flood test, drain camera
  - props

**What we say (verbal)**
- Must have: simple words, clear delivery.
- Nice to have:
  - real numbers
  - a chain of open loops
  - pains and benefits described as the buyer actually lives them

**What they get (script)**
- Must have:
  - call out the prospect
  - a compelling reason why
  - **four comparisons**:
    - the dream outcome vs the nightmare of staying the same
    - speed to result vs the cost of delay
    - certainty of the new path vs the risk of the current one
    - ease vs the difficulty of the current path
  - **as many proof points as possible**
- Nice to have:
  - an admission that costs us something (the "damaging admission")
  - stakes, struggles, investment

**How to get it (CTA)**
- Must have: **one clear spoken AND on-screen next step**.
- Nice to have:
  - a CTA that mirrors the hook
  - real scarcity or urgency
  - a bonus with a reason why

Book's one-line summary: *promise a result, make it fast, likely and easy; curiosity up front, proof in the middle, a single action at the end.*

## 3. Principles that recur across the frameworks (the book says repetition = proven)
1. **Numbers at the start win**, especially big or specific ones ("Data stack"). DC numbers to collect on one sheet before every shoot:
   - inspections completed
   - 529 Google reviews, 4.9★
   - years in business
   - defects found per average report
   - same-day report rate
   - pages and photos per report
   - HR: leaks found, 5★ on Google
2. **Show the floor, not just the ceiling.** People want to know the big result is *possible* but mostly what's *likely*. Give a typical outcome next to the best one ("Underdog").
   - DC: an average report flags X items vs the worst one we found. Typical saving vs the biggest.
3. **Reason why.** A big offer needs a big reason, or it reads as a gimmick. "They'll only think it's a big deal if you tell them it is."
   - DC: *why* same-day? Because buyers are on contract or auction clocks.
4. **Anchor value against real market prices, then drop** ("Value anchor"). Only with true numbers.
   - DC: what a missed defect costs to fix (only verified figures) vs the $550–$847 inspection price.
5. **Curiosity and open loops drive the click; the landing page does the closing.**
   - Short, proof-heavy ads that earn the click, then congruent landing pages finish the job.
   - This matches our per-ad landing page plan.
6. **Movement beats stillness, and real movement beats computer movement.**
   - Walking towards the camera, rounding a corner, catching a prop, a pattern interrupt in the first second.
   - DC: David walking a site at speed, torch or moisture meter in hand, a pipe or tile thrown into frame.
7. **Old way vs new way** ("You're being lied to").
   - DC: "You're told a pre-purchase report takes days. It doesn't have to."
   - **No builder-bashing:** attack the *practice*, never builders.
8. **Selfish reason why builds trust.** People trust openly stated self-interest.
   - DC: "We want you back for your next property" or "we'd rather you hire us for every stage".
9. **Admit the real limitations; that creates genuine scarcity.**
   - DC: limited inspector slots per day (true), same-day only works if we book you in today.
10. **Show what happens after the click.** Show a fast screen recording of the actual form or booking page in the CTA, which cuts friction.
11. **Recycle proven hooks across industries.** Model the structure, never copy competitors' words.
12. **Big visuals. Big offer. Small words. Short sentences.**

## 5. Hard limits (they override the book)
- No fake scarcity or urgency: only real limits, such as inspector capacity or auction dates.
- No prize or "bribe" promotions without checking ACL and Meta rules.
- No dollar claims, defect statistics or "every defect" claims unless David has verified them.
- Never "VBA". BPC DBU-66182. No builder names, logos or signage. No personal-attribute targeting language.

## 6. Jeremy's ruling on how the handbook fits his scoring (2026-10-12)
- **One referee.** The handbook is a build checklist that feeds Jeremy's rubric; it is not a second rubric. Mapping:
  - the four comparisons → **Directness**
  - proof points and "show the floor" → **Proof**
  - prospect call-out and reason why → **Hook placement** (first 5 seconds)
  - movement, captions, real scarcity and numbers-first already sit inside his existing dimensions
- **Reel dosage** (20–25s local-service ads):
  - **2–3 proof points maximum.**
  - Use only the **1–2 comparisons the hook implies** (A2 = certainty vs risk; B2 = cost of delay). The landing page carries the rest.
- **Overrides for our markets:**
  - **Directness closes the click**, not curiosity. Curiosity hooks on a $550 inspection attract tyre-kickers and corrupt the Lead signal. The ad wins the click directly; the page converts the lead.
  - **Presenter = the expert** (David, BPC registered), not a peer of the buyer. Real site B-roll supplies the movement.
  - **"Floor" = price transparency + process clarity**: fixed price by size, and what happens after the click.
  - **Highest-weighted handbook principle for us: show what happens after the click.** Screen-record the form in the CTA. At A$233 CPL, cutting post-click abandonment beats hook tweaks.
  - **Anchor against the cost of NOT inspecting** (a verified five-figure defect after settlement), not against competitor fees.
- **Testing:**
  - A2, B2 and fixed A1 stay as the **control**. Don't rebuild ads that haven't spent; build handbook-style variants to test against them.
  - Launch, collect 72h of signal, then run the Kaleidoscope: 80% variants of the winner, 20% new handbook concepts.
  - **Variants need genuinely different messaging angles.** Near-identical clones get punished by Meta ranking and pile up rejections, which hurts account trust.

## 7. Extra rules from Gym and ALAN (pp26–66)
- **Show the buyer their own world in the first frame**, e.g. a gym owner in a gym. "So simple, yet few do it." For DC: an open home, an auction crowd, a slab or frame stage in a new estate.
- **Proof the viewer can relate to their own situation beats proof from far away.** Use Melbourne suburbs and local estates, never generic stock.
- **Many people moving at once (in unison) stops the scroll.**
- **Specificity proves expertise:** "think moments". Name problems so precise the viewer thinks "he's an insider". Example: the tiler's waterproofing membrane not dry before the tiles go on.
- **Speak to the advanced customer too.** The handbook says to optimise for LTV:CAC, not cheap leads. For DC that means full-stage-package buyers and repeat investors. This **matches Jeremy's booked-job conditioning plan**.
- **Name your mechanism something non-obvious** ("reverse royalty", not "pay per show"). DC idea, needs Jeremy's input: name the same-day process.
- **Preview the next step in the CTA** ("Fill the form → we call within 5 minutes → you get a fixed price and a time"). Detailing the next steps removes friction.
- **Peer endorsement beats self-endorsement for advanced buyers.** Have a real client issue the invite.
- **Testimonial brief** (from Gym #3), six points to ask a client to cover:
  1. personal life before
  2. professional life before
  3. scepticism
  4. why they acted anyway
  5. personal life after
  6. professional life after
